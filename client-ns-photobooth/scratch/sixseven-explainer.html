●ANIMPICKER GESTURE● client-ns-photobooth

Palms up, tilted apart:
6 on one hand, 7 on the other.

The "67" character has no dedicated gesture model. It reads the same 21-point hand landmarks every other palm-triggered prop uses, and decides "diagonal enough, which side is which, whose hand is whose" with geometry alone — five stages, worked through below with the actual thresholds from anim/sixseven.ts.

STAGE 1Signal

Wherever a palm faces the sky, that's a candidate

Every hand the backend reports arrives as 21 normalized landmarks, plus two pre-classified booleans: palmUp (an upright "stop" hand) and palmSky (a flat, outstretched, offering hand). The Drone character already uses palmSky for its hovering prop, so 67 reuses the exact same signal and the exact same landmark math — a person doesn't have to learn a new hand shape, they just need two of the gesture the booth already knows.

A palm's center is the average of the wrist and the four knuckle bases — stable under rotation, unlike the wrist alone, which only works while the hand is held upright.

STAGE 2Matching

Two phases, because the backend hands over no identities

The backend reports a flat list of palms — up to eight, its own confidence ceiling — with no notion of which two belong to the same person. Re-pairing that list from scratch every frame would be unstable: the reported set churns as confidence shifts, and a pair's midpoint alone can't tell two correct hands from two wrong ones that happen to average to the same spot. So a slot that's already showing a 6/7 pair reclaims its own two hands first, by proximity, before any new pairing is attempted.

Only the palms nobody has reclaimed enter the pairing stage. There, every remaining combination is checked against three gates — a plausible hand-to-hand spread, a maximum distance, and the diagonal angle from Stage 3 — and the closest valid combination wins, not the most diagonal one. Ranking by steepest angle would reward exactly the wrong case: my raised hand paired with a stranger's lowered one.

STAGE 3The angle gate

"Diagonal" is a window, not a switch

The tilt between two palms is measured as an angle, not a raw pixel gap — a gap in pixels shrinks for anyone standing further from the camera, but the angle of two outstretched arms doesn't. Below 12° reads as level. Above 72° reads as two different people at two different depths, not one person's arms. Between 20° and 72° is unambiguously a real 67 pose.

The 12–20° band only matters to a pair that's already on screen: it takes a clear 20° tilt to start a pair, but only 12° to keep one alive — enough hysteresis that hovering near the boundary doesn't flicker the numbers in and out.

A detection gap is treated differently from a pair that's simply gone level: losing sight of a hand for a moment is forgiven for 0.4s hold, but a pair that's still tracked and has straightened out fades immediately — the gesture is over, not glitching.

STAGE 4Assignment

Which hand gets the six

No handedness label is ever read from the backend — nothing else in this codebase trusts it, and it's unverified against a mirrored feed. Instead the rule is purely geometric: whichever palm sits at the smaller x on screen becomes the six, the other becomes the seven. Because the feed is mirrored for the guest (the same flip that makes any mirror feel natural), that smaller-x palm is reliably the guest's own left hand.

STAGE 5Lifecycle

Appearing, holding, fading

Once a pair is accepted, both numbers share a single fade state — they rise in together, bob in together, and fade out together, so the two glyphs always read as one prop rather than two independent sprites that happen to be nearby. Losing the diagonal drops a pair straight to fading out, with no grace period — that part of the gesture is meant to end the moment the arms come back level.
